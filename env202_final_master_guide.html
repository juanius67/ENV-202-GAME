M01

Introduction · metabolic classification, redox, thermodynamics

Lecture 1.1 · 75 slides · highest exam weight

Concept summary

Every metabolism is fully described by four things. Memorise these as a checklist — every classification question is a hunt for them in the reaction:

  1. Energy source: chemical (chemo-) or light (photo-)
  2. Electron donor: organic (-organo-) or inorganic (-litho-). Often equal to the energy source for chemotrophs.
  3. Electron acceptor: O₂ (aerobic), other inorganic (anaerobic respiration: NO₃⁻, SO₄²⁻, Fe(III), Mn(IV)), or organic (fermentation).
  4. Carbon source: CO₂/HCO₃⁻ (auto-) or organic (hetero-).

The naming string is energy-electrons-carbon: e.g. chemolithoautotroph = chemical energy, inorganic electrons, CO₂ as C source (think Acidithiobacillus ferrooxidans, nitrifiers).

Redox tower — the most-used reference of the course

Reactions go spontaneously downhill on the tower: e⁻ donor (more negative E⁰′) → e⁻ acceptor (more positive E⁰′). ΔE⁰′ = E⁰′(acceptor) − E⁰′(donor). If ΔE⁰′ > 0, ΔG⁰′ < 0.

Central metabolism scaffolding (must recognise on sight)

  • EMP (glycolysis): glucose(C6) → 2 pyruvate(C3). Yields 2 ATP net, 2 NADH. Universal.
  • CAC/TCA cycle: acetyl-CoA(C2) → 2 CO₂. Generates NADH, FADH₂, GTP. Reverse direction = rTCA (CO₂ fixation).
  • PPP (pentose phosphate pathway): oxidative part = G6P → ribulose-5-P + CO₂ (NADPH); non-oxidative part shuffles C5 sugars → 6C and 3C compounds. Source of NADPH and ribose-5-P (DNA/RNA).
  • Entner-Doudoroff (ED): alternative glycolysis (some Gram-negatives, e.g. Pseudomonas). Only 1 ATP per glucose. Some bacteria have only ED, no EMP.

Exam traps register

Trap 1 — fermentation has the same C as donor AND acceptor. In a table-filling question, if the only "acceptor" you see is also organic and from the same molecule (or its breakdown product), it's fermentation. Energy source = the same organic compound. Examples: lactate → propionate + acetate + CO₂ (lactate is donor, acceptor, energy, C); glucose → butyrate + CO₂ + H₂.
Trap 2 — "energy source" ≠ "electron donor" for phototrophs. For oxygenic PS: energy = light, but e⁻ donor = H₂O. Don't write "light" in the donor cell.
Trap 3 — mixed culture graphs. The organism that drives a transformation is the one whose substrate is being consumed and whose product is being produced. When two compounds change at once, ask which is donor (oxidised) and which is acceptor (reduced) by oxidation-state direction.
Trap 4 — disproportionation. One compound on the left appears as both a more-oxidised and a more-reduced species on the right. Example: 4 S⁰ + 4 H₂O → 3 H₂S + SO₄²⁻ + 2 H⁺ — S⁰ (0) goes to both S(−2) and S(+6). In the classification table, donor = S⁰, acceptor = S⁰.
Trap 5 — F = 96.485 ≈ 96.23 (kJ/mol/V) in lectures. Both pop up in solutions. Use the one stated, but don't be surprised by either. Sign of ΔG⁰′ is the part that matters.
Trap 6 — ΔE⁰′ sign convention. ΔE⁰′ = E⁰′(reduction half) − E⁰′(oxidation half written as reduction). If you flip a couple to write it as oxidation, do NOT flip its E⁰′ sign in this formula.

Drill bank — Intro & metabolism

RECALL Name the four characteristics that fully classify a microbial metabolism.
Answer

(1) Energy source — chemical or light. (2) Electron donor — organic or inorganic. (3) Electron acceptor — O₂, other inorganic, or organic (fermentation). (4) Carbon source — organic or CO₂. Memorise the order: energy / donor / acceptor / carbon.

CONCEPT Distinguish substrate-level from oxidative phosphorylation. Which requires PMF? Which is used by fermenters?
Answer

SLP: ATP made directly during a pathway step (e.g. pyruvate kinase), no PMF, no ATP synthase. Fermenters use only SLP. Oxidative phosphorylation: e⁻ are passed through an ETC, translocating protons across a membrane to build PMF; ATP synthase uses the PMF to make ATP. Respirers (aerobic and anaerobic) use it. Some chemolithotrophs use only oxidative phosphorylation (no fermentation possible).

CONCEPT Why can Acidithiobacillus ferrooxidans be classified as a chemolithoautotroph?
Answer

Energy source = chemical (Fe²⁺ oxidation drives ETC); e⁻ donor = inorganic (Fe²⁺); C source = CO₂ (fixed via Calvin cycle, uses reverse electron flow to generate NADH). Chemo + litho + auto.

CALC For the reaction NO₃⁻ + 5e⁻ + 6H⁺ → ½ N₂ + 3 H₂O (E⁰′ = +0.74 V) coupled to acetate oxidation (CO₂/acetate, E⁰′ = −0.29 V), compute ΔE⁰′ and ΔG⁰′. Per how many moles of acetate or nitrate?
hint
Acetate gives up 8 e⁻; nitrate accepts 5. LCM = 40. Write balanced overall and use n = 40, or report per mole of acetate (n = 8) or per mole of nitrate (n = 5).
Answer

ΔE⁰′ = +0.74 − (−0.29) = +1.03 V.

Per mole of acetate (n = 8): ΔG⁰′ = −8 × 96.485 × 1.03 = −795 kJ/mol acetate.

Per mole of nitrate (n = 5): ΔG⁰′ = −5 × 96.485 × 1.03 = −497 kJ/mol NO₃⁻.

Highly favourable — denitrification with acetate is one of the most energy-yielding anaerobic processes.

CALC Balance and classify: CH₃CHOHCOOH + 4 Fe³⁺ + H₂O → CH₃COOH + CO₂ + 4 Fe²⁺ + 4 H⁺. Identify donor, acceptor, energy source, C source, # e⁻/mol donor, and a likely genus.
hint
Lactate (C3, avg C = 0) → acetate (C2, avg C = 0) + CO₂ (C +4). What's the net change in oxidation state per lactate? How many electrons does that release?
Answer

Lactate: 3 C at avg 0 → total C-ox = 0. Products: acetate (2 C at 0, total 0) + CO₂ (1 C at +4). Net oxidation of C = +4 (one C goes from 0 to +4). That releases 4 e⁻ per lactate. Confirmed: 4 Fe³⁺ accepts 4 e⁻.

Donor: lactate. Acceptor: Fe³⁺. Energy source: lactate. C source: lactate. 4 e⁻/mol lactate. Genus: Shewanella (classic mid-term example from 2024).

CALC Compute the oxidation state of nitrogen in: NH₃, N₂, N₂O, NO, NO₂⁻, NO₃⁻. Then order the N-cycle reactions in increasing oxidation state.
Answer

NH₃ = −3 · N₂ = 0 · N₂O = +1 · NO = +2 · NO₂⁻ = +3 · NO₃⁻ = +5.

Order: NH₃/NH₄⁺ (−3) → N₂ (0) → N₂O (+1) → NO (+2) → NO₂⁻ (+3) → NO₃⁻ (+5). Each step in nitrification adds 1–2 e⁻ removed; each step in denitrification adds 1–2 e⁻ accepted.

INTEG Look at this reaction: 4 S⁰ + 4 H₂O → 3 H₂S + SO₄²⁻ + 2 H⁺. Is it a disproportionation? Justify and fill the metabolism table (donor / acceptor / energy / C / # e⁻).
Answer

Ox states: S⁰ has S = 0. Products: H₂S has S = −2, SO₄²⁻ has S = +6. One compound (S⁰) gives products at both higher and lower ox states → yes, disproportionation.

Donor: S⁰ (the S⁰ that oxidises to SO₄²⁻). Acceptor: S⁰ (the S⁰ that reduces to H₂S). Energy source: S⁰. C source: CO₂ (no organic C in equation — autotrophic). Electrons per H₂S produced: 2 (S 0 → −2). Per overall reaction: 4 S⁰ used → 1 ends up at +6 (loses 6 e⁻), 3 end up at −2 (gain 6 e⁻ together). Balance checks. From HW1: per mole H₂S produced, 2 e⁻ transferred.

INTEG A lake bottle is opened to air under light (phase 1), then sealed in the dark (phase 2), then sealed again under light (phase 3). For each phase, predict what happens to: CO₂, acetate, H₂, chlorophyll/Bchl. Identify a genus for each.
Answer

Phase 1 (light, open, O₂): oxygenic photosynthesis by cyanobacteria. CO₂ stable (in equilibrium with atmosphere). Acetate accumulates slightly (released organic C). Chlorophyll grows. Genus: Anabaena or Synechococcus.

Phase 2 (dark, closed): O₂ depleted by respiration; then fermentation kicks in on the cyanobacterial biomass. CO₂ and H₂ and acetate accumulate (fermentation products); chlorophyll degrades. Genus: Clostridium.

Phase 3 (light, closed, anoxic): anoxygenic photosynthesis. Acetate consumed (e⁻ donor for purple non-sulfur), CO₂ fixed, H₂ consumed (e⁻ donor for purple non-sulfur or green non-sulfur), bacteriochlorophyll appears. Genus: Rhodopseudomonas or Chloroflexus. Directly from midterm 2023.

INTEG Why is biological nitrogen fixation a remarkable process from a thermodynamic and kinetic standpoint? Compare to Haber-Bosch.
Answer

N₂ has a triple bond — kinetically inert; ΔG of N≡N cleavage is enormous; the activation energy is huge. Haber-Bosch requires 200 atm and 450 °C with Fe catalyst. Nitrogenase (an Fe-protein + MoFe-protein complex) does it at 1 atm and 25 °C, but at a steep ATP cost (16 ATP per N₂ for 6 e⁻ delivered — actually 8 e⁻ consumed because H₂ is also produced). The MoFe cofactor's job is to bind and activate N₂ at the Fe atoms. Both nitrogenase components are O₂-sensitive, which is why N-fixers either segregate (heterocysts in Anabaena), separate in time (Synechococcus), or wrap themselves in slime (Azotobacter).

M02

Enzymes · structure, classification, mechanism

Lecture 1.2 · 27 slides · concept-heavy, low calc

Concept summary

An enzyme is a (usually) protein catalyst. It lowers the activation energy of a reaction but does not change its ΔG. Substrates bind the active site; products leave; the enzyme is regenerated. Highly substrate-specific (lactase) or very broad (toluene dioxygenase).

An endergonic reaction (ΔG > 0) can be catalysed by an enzyme by coupling it to an exergonic one so the overall ΔG < 0 (e.g. ATP hydrolysis paired with biosynthesis).

CONCEPT Bacteria convert arsenate (AsO₄³⁻) to arsenite (AsO₃³⁻). Determine the As oxidation state in each, name the enzyme class, and write the balanced half-reaction.
Answer

Arsenate: 4 O at −2 (= −8), 3 H at +1 (= +3), total charge −3. As ox state must satisfy: As + (−8) + (+3) = −3 → As = +5. Arsenite: 3 O at −2 (= −6), 3 H at +1 (= +3), total −3 → As = +3. So As goes from +5 to +3, a reduction (gains 2 e⁻).

Enzyme: oxidoreductase, specifically arsenate reductase.

Half-reaction: AsO₄³⁻ + 2 H⁺ + 2 e⁻ → AsO₃³⁻ + H₂O.

CONCEPT An enzyme catalyses a reaction with ΔG = +15 kJ/mol. Will the reaction proceed? How could a cell make it happen?
Answer

No — enzymes only change the rate, not the thermodynamics. ΔG > 0 means the reaction is endergonic and will not proceed spontaneously. To drive it, the cell couples it to an exergonic reaction (typically ATP hydrolysis, ΔG⁰′ ≈ −32 kJ/mol) so that the overall ΔG_total < 0. This is the basis of ligase activity, biosynthesis pathways, and reverse electron flow in A. ferrooxidans.

CONCEPT Two reactions both use ATP. (a) Glucose + ATP → glucose-6-phosphate + ADP. (b) Benzoate + CoA-SH + ATP → benzoyl-CoA + AMP + PPi. Which is a transferase and which a ligase? Justify.
Answer

(a) is a transferase (hexokinase) — a phosphate from ATP ends up in the product (glucose-6-phosphate). The functional group is being transferred.

(b) is a ligase — ATP is hydrolysed (to AMP + PPi, indicating two phosphoanhydride bonds broken — extra-strong drive) only as an energy source; no ATP fragment is in benzoyl-CoA. The bond formed is between benzoate and CoA.

CONCEPT 1,2-dichloroethane → ... → glycolate. Two replacements of Cl by OH (with H₂O, releasing HCl) bracket two oxidation steps. Classify each enzyme.
Answer

From HW2: Step 1 (Cl replaced by OH using H₂O, releasing HCl): hydrolase. Step 2 (loss of 2 [H], i.e. oxidation): oxidoreductase. Step 3 (another oxidation, 2 [H] removed): oxidoreductase. Step 4 (last Cl → OH with H₂O, releases HCl): hydrolase. Recognising oxidation vs hydrolysis is the whole skill: look at H, OH balance and oxidation-state change.

RECALL What is the difference between primary, secondary, tertiary, and quaternary protein structure? Give a course example of a multi-subunit enzyme.
Answer

Primary: linear amino-acid sequence (polypeptide). Secondary: local folds (α-helices, β-sheets). Tertiary: full 3D fold of a single polypeptide, stabilised by H-bonds, disulfide bridges, hydrophobic interactions. Quaternary: assembly of multiple folded polypeptide subunits into a functional protein. Example: Nar (nitrate reductase) has three subunits — NarG (catalytic), NarH (Fe-S cluster), NarI (membrane anchor).

INTEG The first step of methanogenesis is CO₂ + MF → CHO-MF (formyl-MF). Identify the enzyme class. Then, for the methanogenesis sequence CHO-MF → CH≡H₄MP → CH₂=H₄MP → CH₃-H₄MP → CH₃-CoM → CH₄, classify each step (oxidoreductase, transferase, etc.).
Answer

CO₂ + MF → CHO-MF: this is a reduction (C goes +4 → +2) — but also forms a new C-N bond. The C goes from CO₂ to MF, and it's reduced. This is an oxidoreductase (formyl-methanofuran dehydrogenase, catalyses reductive CO₂ activation).

CHO-MF → CH≡H₄MP: methyl group is transferred from MF to MP, no redox → transferase.

CH≡H₄MP → CH₂=H₄MP: reduction by F420H₂ (one C, +2 → 0) → oxidoreductase.

CH₂=H₄MP → CH₃-H₄MP: reduction (C 0 → −2) → oxidoreductase.

CH₃-H₄MP → CH₃-CoM: methyl transferred from H₄MP to CoM (this drives Na⁺ translocation but is still chemically a methyl transfer) → transferase.

CH₃-CoM → CH₄: reduction by HS-CoB releasing CoM-S-S-CoB; C −2 → −4 → oxidoreductase (methyl-CoM reductase, gene mcr). Recognising this matters because mcr is the marker gene for AOM too.

CONCEPT Why are enzymes essential for catalysing endergonic biosynthesis (e.g. nitrogen fixation)? Explain in terms of coupling.
Answer

Endergonic reactions don't proceed spontaneously (ΔG > 0). Enzymes don't change ΔG, so they cannot drive the reaction alone. A multi-step enzyme catalyses an endergonic step coupled to a strongly exergonic step (typically ATP→ADP+Pi at −32 kJ/mol). For N fixation: 16 ATP hydrolysed per N₂ — that's the coupling. The net ΔG of the coupled process is negative, and the nitrogenase enzyme catalyses both halves in concert at its FeMo cofactor.

M03

Enzyme kinetics & chemostat · Michaelis-Menten, inhibition, microbial growth

Lecture 2 · 37 slides · highest-calc topic; appears every year

Concept summary

For a one-substrate enzyme reaction E + S ⇌ ES → E + P, the rate is set by [S]:

Michaelis-Menten equationv = v_max · [S] / (K_M + [S])
  • K_M = the substrate concentration at which v = v_max/2. Inversely related to enzyme's affinity. Low K_M → high affinity. Units = concentration (M, mol/L, mg/L).
  • v_max = max rate, achieved when [S] ≫ K_M (all enzyme is in ES form). Equals k_cat · [E]_tot.
  • k_cat (turnover number) = max # substrate molecules transformed per active site per second. Units = 1/time.
  • k_cat / K_M = catalytic efficiency. Diffusion limit ≈ 10⁸–10⁹ M⁻¹ s⁻¹ — enzymes near this are "kinetically perfect" (e.g. catalase, carbonic anhydrase).

At environmental [S] (well below K_M): v ≈ (v_max / K_M) · [S]. This is a second-order reaction — first order in both [E] and [S].

Exam traps register (Juan-specific from midterm)

Trap 1 — methanol MW = 32, not 16. Burned by this last year. Always sanity-check: count atoms.
Trap 2 — S_min uses b directly. The exam form is S_min = b·K_S/(μ_max − b). Don't substitute b ≈ m·Y_g; just plug b as given.
Trap 3 — K_S unit conversion. K_S given in mg/L, μ_max in h⁻¹: dimensionless ratio (K_S·D/(μ_max−D)) keeps mg/L. Don't mix with mol/L unless you convert.
Trap 4 — μ = D only at steady state. In transient, μ ≠ D. Most exam questions assume steady state but always check.
Trap 5 — μ_max sets the wash-out limit, not the operational D. A bioreactor with μ_max = 0.1 h⁻¹ cannot be run with θ_h = 4 h (D = 0.25 h⁻¹) — wash-out. Worked example in HW3 P6.
Trap 6 — yield Y vs μ. Y = ΔX/ΔS (gram biomass per gram substrate). q_S = μ/Y is the specific substrate consumption rate. Don't confuse with v_max.
Trap 7 — COD ≠ mass of substrate. COD measures electrons (oxygen demand) — for ethanol, 1 g COD = 0.48 g ethanol. Convert COD limit to actual substrate concentration before solving.

Drill bank — Kinetics & chemostat

RECALL Define K_M, v_max, and k_cat. What are their units?
Answer

K_M: substrate concentration at half-max rate; units = concentration (M, mg/L). Inversely related to affinity. v_max: max rate at saturating [S]; units = concentration/time or substrate/(enzyme·time). k_cat: v_max / [E]_tot; max substrates processed per active site per unit time; units = 1/time. The ratio k_cat/K_M = catalytic efficiency, units = M⁻¹·s⁻¹.

CONCEPT Two enzymes work on the same substrate. Enzyme A has K_M = 1 mM, v_max = 10 µmol/min. Enzyme B has K_M = 0.01 mM, v_max = 2 µmol/min. In an environment where [S] = 0.05 mM, which is more efficient? Justify.
Answer

At [S] = 0.05 mM: Enzyme A: [S] ≪ K_M (1 mM), so v_A ≈ (v_max/K_M)·[S] = (10/1)·0.05 = 0.5 µmol/min. Enzyme B: [S] = 5·K_M, well above K_M, so v_B ≈ v_max·[S]/(K_M+[S]) = 2·0.05/(0.01+0.05) = 1.67 µmol/min. Enzyme B is more efficient at this [S] because of its much higher affinity. This is why in environmental microbiology, K_M (or K_S) often matters more than v_max — substrate concentrations are typically low.

CALC An enzyme with K_M = 5 mM and v_max = 100 µmol/min is incubated with [S] = 2 mM and an inhibitor [I] = 0.5 mM with K_I = 0.1 mM. The inhibitor is competitive. Calculate v.
hint
Competitive: replace K_M by K_M · (1 + [I]/K_I). v_max stays.
Answer

K_M,app = 5 · (1 + 0.5/0.1) = 5 · 6 = 30 mM. v = 100 · 2 / (30 + 2) = 100 · 2/32 = 6.25 µmol/min. (Without inhibitor: v = 100 · 2/7 = 28.6 — large decrease, characteristic of competitive inhibition at sub-saturating [S].)

CONCEPT On a Lineweaver-Burk plot, lines with and without inhibitor cross on the x-axis (i.e. same −1/K_M) but have different y-intercepts. Which inhibition type is this?
Answer

Same x-intercept = same K_M. Different y-intercept = different v_max. This is non-competitive inhibition. The inhibitor binds an allosteric site whether or not substrate is present, so it doesn't compete for the active site (K_M unchanged) but it removes some enzyme from action regardless of [S] (v_max ↓).

CALC A chemostat treats ethanol-containing wastewater. The effluent limit is 20 mg COD/L. (1 g COD = 0.48 g ethanol.) Biomass: μ_max = 0.1 h⁻¹, K_S = 40 mg ethanol/L. Find the required hydraulic residence time θ_h.

Answer

20 mg COD/L × 0.48 = 9.6 mg ethanol/L (this is the maximum allowable S in the effluent).

θ_h = 1/D = ≈ 52 h ≈ 2 days. (HW3 P7.)

CALC A chemostat: μ_max = 5 h⁻¹, Y = 0.5 g/g, K_S = 0.5 mg/L, S₀ = 20 mg/L. Compute the biomass X at D = 2 h⁻¹.
Answer

S = K_S·D/(μ_max−D) = 0.5·2/(5−2) = 1/3 ≈ 0.333 mg/L.

X = Y·(S₀ − S) = 0.5·(20 − 0.333) = 0.5·19.667 = ≈ 9.83 mg/L = 9.83 g/m³.

CALC A culture grows from 10³ cells to 10⁶ cells in 5 h. Compute growth rate k (h⁻¹) and generation time.

log(N_t) − log(N_0) = n · log 2, where n = number of generations.
n = (log 10⁶ − log 10³)/log 2 = (6 − 3)/0.301 = 9.97 ≈ 10 generations.

CALC A bacterium has b = 0.02 h⁻¹, K_S = 5 mg/L, μ_max = 0.2 h⁻¹. What is its S_min?

INTEG A chemostat at steady state. The dilution rate is doubled. Predict: (a) what happens to [S] in the effluent, (b) what happens to biomass, (c) what could go wrong.
Answer

(a) S = K_S·D/(μ_max−D) → as D increases, S increases (less time for cells to consume substrate). At some point S approaches K_S; near wash-out, S → S₀. (b) X = Y·(S₀ − S) → as S increases, X decreases. (c) If 2D ≥ μ_max, wash-out: cells leave faster than they can grow. The new steady state would be X = 0, S = S₀.

INTEG Methanotroph in chemostat (mid-term 2025 Q1 style). Given μ_max, K_S, b, S₀ for Methylotuvimicrobium on methanol, compute: (a) doubling time, (b) S_min, (c) outlet S at D = μ_max/2. What's the yield Y if at D = μ_max/2 the biomass is X g/L?

Drill bank — Fermentation

RECALL Why does fermentation yield ~2 ATP per glucose vs ~30+ for respiration?
Answer

Fermentation uses only substrate-level phosphorylation — ATP made directly at pathway steps (e.g. pyruvate kinase). No ETC, no PMF, no ATP synthase. Respiration adds oxidative phosphorylation: ~10 NADH × 3 ATP + 2 FADH₂ × 2 ATP per fully-oxidised glucose. Trade-off: fermentation needs no external e⁻ acceptor.

CONCEPT A flask contains soil + sugar, sealed. After a week: lactate present, no propionate, no butyrate, no ethanol. What metabolism, what organism?
Answer

Homolactic fermentation (glucose → 2 lactate). Likely Lactobacillus. Absence of secondary products = no secondary fermenter present (or lactate not yet consumed).

Mixed-acid acetate: chemoorganoheterotrophic. Donor = glucose, acceptor = glucose, energy = glucose, C source = glucose. SLP only. E. coli. Same chemical product, totally different table entries.

INTEG Cow rumen network (HW5 P3): given Methanobacterium, Methanosarcina, Propionibacterium, Lactobacillus, Clostridium, sketch the consumption-production chain from glucose to CH₄.

Hydrogenotrophic methanogens (Methanobacterium) consume H₂ + CO₂ → CH₄. Low CO₂ = consumed by hydrogenotrophs faster than acetoclasts release it. Fe(III) reduction · breathing rocks, Shewanella vs Geobacter

Lecture 4 part 1 · 25 slides

Concept summary

Fe(III) reduction = anaerobic respiration. Fe(III) is the e⁻ acceptor, an organic compound (acetate, lactate) or H₂ is the donor. The challenge: at neutral pH, Fe(III) is highly insoluble (Fe(OH)₃, goethite, hematite, magnetite — all solids). Cells must transfer e⁻ from inside the cytoplasm to a solid mineral outside. "Bacteria breathing rocks."

Five strategies to deliver e⁻ to solid Fe(III)

  2. Conductive pili (microbial nanowires). Geobacter sulfurreducens: OmcS cytochromes spaced along pili for e⁻ hopping to distant Fe(III).
  3. Electron shuttles. Excrete diffusible redox-active small molecules (e.g. flavins) — reduced at cell, oxidised at mineral, recycled.
  4. Fe(III) chelators. Produce ligands (NTA, citrate, EDTA) that solubilise Fe(III) as Fe(III)-L complexes.
  5. Biofilm electron hopping. Cell-to-cell relay through cytochromes.

With lactate, no reduction — lactate oxidation doesn't produce a diffusible carrier; requires direct cell-mineral contact.

RECALL List the five mechanisms Fe(III)-reducing bacteria use to deliver e⁻ to solid Fe(III).
Answer

(1) Direct cell-mineral contact via outer-mem c-type cytochromes. (2) Conductive pili / microbial nanowires. (3) Excreted electron shuttles (e.g. flavins). (4) Fe(III) chelators (e.g. NTA-like ligands) that solubilise Fe(III). (5) Multistep e⁻ hopping in biofilm.

CONCEPT Why is Fe(III) reduction at neutral pH inherently harder than O₂ reduction?
Answer

O₂ is a small soluble molecule that diffuses into the cell to the inner-membrane terminal oxidase. Fe(III) at neutral pH is essentially insoluble (Fe(OH)₃ solubility <1 nM at pH 7), present as solid minerals that cannot enter the periplasm — let alone the cytoplasm. Cells must extend their respiratory chain to the outer surface and use exotic strategies (nanowires, shuttles, chelators). Below pH ~3, Fe(III) is soluble — that's why acidophilic Fe reducers (Acidiphilium) don't need these tricks.

Lecture 4 part 2 · 22 slides · A. ferrooxidans diagram is high-yield

Concept summary

Fe(II) oxidation = energy generation by oxidising Fe²⁺ → Fe³⁺. Donor = Fe(II), and the metabolism depends on environmental conditions (pH, O₂). Three categories of Fe(II) oxidisers:

GroupConditionsOrganismsKey challenge
1. Aerobic acidophilespH ~0–2, oxicAcidithiobacillus ferrooxidans, Leptospirillum ferrooxidans, FerroplasmaVery low ΔE⁰′ (only ~0.06 V); E of Fe couple is too high to reduce NAD⁺

Group 1: A. ferrooxidans — the dual ETC (the most-asked diagram)

The thermodynamic problem: at pH 2, Fe³⁺/Fe²⁺ has E⁰ ≈ +0.76 V. O₂/H₂O has E⁰ ≈ +0.82 V. ΔE⁰ ≈ 0.06 V — barely energy-positive. Worse: the Fe(II) potential is far above NAD⁺/NADH (−0.32 V), so e⁻ from Fe(II) cannot reduce NAD⁺ directly. Yet the organism is autotrophic — needs NADH for CO₂ fixation (Calvin cycle).

Solution: two electron flows operating in parallel.

  • Downhill path (energy generation): Fe²⁺ → rusticyanin (E⁰′ = +0.68 V) → cyt c (Cyc2, +0.56 V) → cyt aa₃ oxidase → O₂. Small ΔE = small energy yield. The reduction of O₂ consumes 4 H⁺ from cytoplasm, helping maintain near-neutral cytoplasmic pH despite acidic environment.
  • Uphill path (NADH generation by reverse electron flow, ATP-driven): Fe²⁺ → cyt c → cyt bc₁ complex → quinone → Fe-S protein → flavoprotein → NAD⁺ + 2 H⁺ → NADH. This goes against the redox gradient, requires PMF (and thus ATP). The reverse direction of the normal aerobic respiratory chain.
Why so much Fe(II) → Fe(III) for so little biomass. Each Fe²⁺ delivers only 1 e⁻ with very little energy. Add the cost of reverse electron flow for NADH (each NADH costs more than 1 ATP equivalent). Net: organism oxidises huge amounts of Fe(II) for minimal growth. This is why acid mine drainage produces massive Fe(III) precipitates — biology is just doing what it can with what it has.

Other adaptations: acid-stable enzymes (rusticyanin, Cyc2, high-potential cytochromes). Fe³⁺ stays soluble at pH 2, so no Fe(OH)₃ precipitation on the outer membrane. ATP made by H⁺ entry from outside (cytoplasm at pH ~6 — huge gradient = large PMF naturally).

Group 2: aerobic neutrophiles

  • Live at oxic-anoxic interfaces (e.g. anoxic groundwater seeping into oxic water).
  • Better energetics than acidophiles: at pH 6–7, Fe(II) is often Fe(II)-CO₃ (siderite), Fe(III) is Fe(OH)₃ — couple E⁰ ≈ +0.1–0.2 V, much lower than at pH 2; ΔE with O₂ couple is larger.
  • Encrustation problem: Fe(III) precipitates immediately at neutral pH; cells could be entombed. Two strategies:
    • Gallionella grows on a stalk — Fe oxides deposit on the stalk, cells stay at the tip uncoated.
    • Leptothrix grows inside a sheath — Fe oxides deposit on empty sheaths (~91 % are empty!), cells generally inside.

Exam traps register

Trap 1 — at acidic pH, Fe(II)/Fe(III) E⁰ ≈ +0.76 V; at neutral pH ≈ +0.1–0.2 V. The same redox couple has wildly different E⁰′ depending on which solid phase predominates (Fe(OH)₃ vs Fe(CO)₃ vs soluble Fe²⁺/³⁺). Don't reuse the acidic value at neutral pH.
Trap 2 — reverse electron flow is the diagnostic feature. Whenever an organism's e⁻ donor has E⁰′ > NAD⁺/NADH (−0.32 V), it must use reverse e⁻ flow to make NADH — and that costs PMF/ATP. A. ferrooxidans, nitrifiers (NO₂⁻/NO₃⁻), purple bacteria (anoxygenic PS).
Trap 3 — abiotic vs biotic Fe(II) oxidation. At high O₂ and neutral pH, Fe(II) oxidises spontaneously in seconds — no organism needed. Biological Fe(II) oxidation requires niches: low O₂ (neutrophilic aerobic), acidic pH (acidophilic aerobic), or anoxic (phototrophic / NO₃⁻-driven).
Trap 4 — Gallionella stalks ≠ pili nanowires. Different organism, different function. Stalks are extracellular ribbons of polysaccharide where Fe(OH)₃ precipitates, keeping cells from being encrusted. Pili (Geobacter) are conductive structures for e⁻ transfer.
Trap 5 — bioleaching writes Fe(III) as both product and reactant. Fe(III) is the working oxidant; the bacteria regenerate it from Fe(II) using O₂. So Fe(III) consumes CuS (reaction 2) and regenerates from Fe(II) (reaction 3). Don't be confused by Fe appearing on both sides of the overall scheme.

CONCEPT Why does A. ferrooxidans oxidise so much Fe(II) for so little biomass?
Answer

The ΔE⁰ between Fe(II)/Fe(III) (+0.76 V at pH 2) and O₂/H₂O (+0.82 V) is only ~0.06 V — tiny. Each Fe²⁺ delivers only 1 e⁻ and only ~5–6 kJ/mol of energy. On top of that, the organism is autotrophic, so it must run reverse electron flow to make NADH for CO₂ fixation — that consumes ATP. Net: enormous Fe turnover for minimal growth. This is why AMD environments produce massive Fe oxide precipitates while bacterial biomass remains modest.

CONCEPT Explain why A. ferrooxidans cannot reduce NAD⁺ via the normal forward electron transport chain.
Answer

The e⁻ donor (Fe²⁺) sits at E⁰′ ≈ +0.76 V. NAD⁺/NADH sits at E⁰′ = −0.32 V. Electrons spontaneously flow downhill (high to low E). Reducing NAD⁺ from Fe²⁺ would require electrons to go uphill by ~1.08 V — impossible spontaneously. So the cell uses reverse electron flow: pulls electrons backward up the ETC through cyt bc₁, quinones, Fe-S proteins, and a flavoprotein to NAD⁺, powered by PMF. Net energy cost: large fraction of the ATP generated downhill.

CONCEPT How does Gallionella avoid being encrusted in Fe(III) precipitates at neutral pH?

Cell rotation twists the stalk. As the cell divides and continues growing, the stalk lengthens and branches. The mature cells stay at the growing tips, free of mineral encasement. M07

Photosynthesis · oxygenic vs anoxygenic, Type I vs Type II reaction centres

Lecture 5 · 33 slides

Concept summary

Phototrophy = using light as energy source. Two flavours:

  • Oxygenic photosynthesis: produces O₂. Performed by cyanobacteria, algae, plants. Uses H₂O as electron donor (the only natural e⁻ donor with a higher E⁰′ than O₂/H₂O — needs special "Z scheme").
  • Anoxygenic photosynthesis: no O₂. Performed by purple sulfur, purple non-sulfur, green sulfur, green non-sulfur bacteria (and heliobacteria). Uses H₂S, H₂, Fe²⁺, or organic compounds as e⁻ donors.

How photosynthesis works mechanistically

  1. Light excites a pigment (chlorophyll or bacteriochlorophyll) in a reaction centre (RC) — converts P → P*.
  2. P* is a much better e⁻ donor than P (excited state has very negative E⁰′).
  3. P* gives its electron to the ETC, generating PMF and ATP.
  4. P is regenerated by an external e⁻ donor (H₂O, H₂S, etc.) or by cyclic flow.

Why cyanobacteria need two photosystems (the Z scheme)

H₂O is a poor electron donor — its E⁰′ (+0.82 V) is the highest of any natural donor. Yet cyanobacteria use H₂O to make NADH (E⁰′ = −0.32 V), requiring an uphill jump of ~1.14 V. One single light excitation isn't enough to push electrons that far. Solution: two photosystems in series. PS II oxidises water and pushes electrons partway up. They flow downhill through plastoquinone/cyt b₆f, generating ATP. Then PS I picks them up and pushes them up again, this time high enough to reduce NADP⁺ via ferredoxin. The shape of this energy profile = the letter Z.

Trap 1 — energy source for photosynthesis is LIGHT, not the e⁻ donor. In oxygenic PS, e⁻ donor = H₂O, energy source = light. Don't conflate.
Trap 2 — RC type ≠ phylum. Green sulfur (Chlorobi) uses RC I. Green NON-sulfur (Chloroflexi) uses RC II. Despite both being "green", their reaction centres differ — and Chloroflexus uses 3-HP bi-cycle (not rTCA).

Trap 5 — chlorosomes vs phycobilisomes. Chlorosomes = giant light-harvesting antennae in green bacteria (~1000 light-harvesting centres per RC; adaptation to low light). Phycobilisomes = surface antennae in cyanobacteria (chromophores phycoerythrin, phycocyanin, allophycocyanin).
Trap 6 — microbial mat stratification. CONCEPT Why is oxygenic photosynthesis a "Z scheme"? What does the Z stand for?
Answer

The Z refers to the shape of the energy diagram when redox potential is plotted (with high E⁰′ at top). H₂O is at +0.82 V (top right). PS II light excitation jumps electrons up to a very negative state (P680*, ~−0.7 V). They flow downhill through PQ and cyt b₆f back up to PS I (P700, +0.5 V). PS I light excitation jumps electrons up again (P700*, ~−1.0 V). They reduce ferredoxin (~−0.4 V), then NADP⁺ (−0.32 V). The diagram traces a Z. Two light excitations are needed because no single photon-induced excitation could span from H₂O (+0.82) to NADP⁺ (−0.32).

Key mechanistic distinctions

  • Calvin (CBB): closed cycle. Each turn fixes 1 CO₂ onto ribulose-1,5-biP (RuBP, C5) via RubisCO → two 3-phosphoglycerate (C3) molecules. Reduced and rearranged to regenerate RuBP. After 3 turns: net 1 G3P (C3) for biosynthesis. RubisCO is famously slow and has an oxygenase side reaction (photorespiration in plants). Cyanobacteria carry RubisCO inside carboxysomes — protein shells that concentrate CO₂ to outcompete the oxygenase reaction.
  • rTCA: TCA cycle run backwards. Three key enzymes that differ from forward TCA: α-ketoglutarate synthase (vs α-KG dehydrogenase) reduces succinyl-CoA to α-KG + adds CO₂; ATP-citrate lyase (vs citrate synthase) splits citrate → OAA + acetyl-CoA; PFOR (pyruvate:ferredoxin oxidoreductase) reduces acetyl-CoA to pyruvate + adds CO₂. Highly reducing — uses ferredoxin (~−0.4 V). Found in obligate anaerobes that get electrons via RC I (linear) photosynthesis or chemolithotrophy.
  • Wood-Ljungdahl (WL): not a cycle — a pathway (linear). Two arms converge: (1) CO₂ reduced stepwise to a methyl group bound to tetrahydrofolate (THF); (2) CO₂ reduced to a carbonyl bound to a Ni-Fe centre in CODH (carbon monoxide dehydrogenase). The methyl and carbonyl combine on CODH to form acetyl-CoA. PFOR then makes pyruvate. CODH is O₂-extreme-sensitive → only in strict anaerobes. Marker genes: cooS, acsB.
  • 3-HP bi-cycle: cycle within cycle. 2× acetyl-CoA carboxylated to propionyl-CoA (common stem). First cycle converts propionyl-CoA → malyl-CoA + acetyl-CoA + glyoxylate. Second cycle uses propionyl-CoA + glyoxylate → citramalyl-CoA → pyruvate + acetyl-CoA. Marker: mch (mesaconyl hydratase).
  • 3-HP/4-HB and DC/4-HB: archaeal pathways with thermotolerance, low metal needs, use HCO₃⁻. Both produce acetyl-CoA via 4-hydroxybutyrate intermediate. DC/HB uses ferredoxin (anaerobic); HP/HB uses NADH (aerobic).

• Cyanobacteria, plants, purple bacteria, some proteobacteria → Calvin

CONCEPT Why is the Wood-Ljungdahl pathway "the cheapest" CO₂ fixation pathway energetically?
Answer

It requires only ~1 ATP per pyruvate, vs 7 for Calvin. It's a linear pathway (no expensive cycle to maintain). It directly couples CO₂ reduction to electron donors at very low E⁰′ (H₂, ferredoxin). It doesn't have a wasteful side reaction like RubisCO's oxygenase. It uses CODH, a remarkably efficient bifunctional enzyme (CO₂ reduction + acetyl-CoA synthesis in one centre). The trade-off: O₂ destroys CODH, so only strict anaerobes can use it.

INTEG A newly discovered CO₂ fixation mechanism (midterm 2024 Q1): 6 steps, ends at pyruvate. Pathway, not cycle. No intermediate is regenerated; the inputs (CO₂, NH₃, ATP, electrons) are consumed linearly to produce pyruvate. THF is recycled but it's a co-factor, not an intermediate. Most resembles Wood-Ljungdahl: both are pathways (not cycles); both use THF as a C1 carrier; both end with acetyl-CoA, then PFOR to pyruvate; both require very reducing conditions; both use CODH-like chemistry (here, "glycine synthase + glycine reductase" combine the methyl group with another C source). CONCEPT What is a carboxysome and why does it exist?
Answer

A carboxysome is a polyhedral protein microcompartment found in cyanobacteria (and some chemolithoautotrophs). It contains RubisCO and carbonic anhydrase. The shell concentrates CO₂ around RubisCO and excludes O₂. This counters the inefficiency of RubisCO, which has an oxygenase side reaction with O₂ that wastes energy (photorespiration in plants). By raising local [CO₂]/[O₂], the carboxysome shifts RubisCO toward productive carboxylation.

CONCEPT Why does Chlorobium (green sulfur bacterium) use rTCA rather than Calvin?
Answer

Chlorobium performs anoxygenic photosynthesis with RC I (P840), which reduces ferredoxin directly via linear electron flow. Ferredoxin is the key reductant for rTCA's critical steps (α-ketoglutarate synthase, PFOR). So rTCA matches the available reductant. It also costs less ATP than Calvin (2 vs 7 per pyruvate), which suits an anaerobic phototroph where ATP is limited. Plus rTCA's intermediates (acetyl-CoA, pyruvate) are useful biosynthetic precursors. Calvin needs lots of NADPH that Chlorobium would have to make expensively.

INTEG Anammox bacteria fix CO₂ via the acetyl-CoA pathway. Why is this consistent with their metabolism, and what role does nitrate play in their CO₂ fixation?
Answer

Anammox bacteria (Brocadia) are strict anaerobes — anammox happens in the anammoxosome under anoxic conditions. WL is the only CO₂ fixation pathway available to strict anaerobes that's energy-cheap, and it produces acetyl-CoA + pyruvate as biosynthetic stems. For electrons: anammox bacteria use Nxr (nitrite oxidoreductase) running in reverse: NO₂⁻ → NO₃⁻ + 2 e⁻. So nitrite serves two roles: (a) electron acceptor for NH₄⁺ oxidation (the core anammox catabolic reaction), and (b) electron donor for CO₂ fixation, because oxidising NO₂⁻ to NO₃⁻ releases the electrons needed by WL/CODH.

Concept summary

Methane is the second-most-abundant biogenic carbon gas. About half the CH₄ produced microbially is oxidised before reaching the atmosphere. Three relevant processes:

Methanogenesis

  • Performed by Archaea only (not Bacteria). Genera: Methanobacterium, Methanosarcina, Methanococcus, Methanopyrus, Methanocaldococcus.
  • Strict anaerobes. Use novel enzymes and cofactors.
  • No SLP — energy from translocating ions (H⁺ and Na⁺) via membrane complexes.

Methanogenesis enzymology — the coenzymes you must name

  • Methanofuran (MF): binds CO₂ at amino N, makes formyl-MF.
  • Methanopterin (H₄MP): similar to THF but harder to reduce; carries C₁ through reductive stages (formyl, methenyl, methylene, methyl).
  • Coenzyme M (HS-CoM): receives the methyl group at penultimate step. Thiol active group; sulfonate gives water solubility.
  • Coenzyme B (HS-CoB): provides final reducing equivalents. The heterodisulfide CoM-S-S-CoB is the product, recycled by Hdr complex.
  • F₄₃₀: Ni-tetrapyrrole at the active site of methyl-CoM reductase (the mcr enzyme).
  • F₄₂₀: flavin-like coenzyme. Oxidised form absorbs at 420 nm and fluoresces blue-green. Carries 2 e⁻.

The diagnostic gene: mcr

Methyl-CoM reductase catalyses the last step: CH₃-S-CoM + HS-CoB → CH₄ + CoM-S-S-CoB. It's the ONLY enzyme common to all three methanogenesis types — so mcr is the marker gene for methanogenesis. The same enzyme run in reverse catalyses AOM in ANME archaea — so mcr is also the marker for AOM.

Methanotrophy (aerobic CH₄ oxidation)

  • Performed by methanotrophs, a subset of methylotrophs. Aerobic bacteria, Proteobacteria.
  • Energy + carbon source = CH₄. Use O₂ as e⁻ acceptor.
  • Two types differ in how they assimilate C₁:
    • Type I: ribulose monophosphate (RuMP) pathway. Gammaproteobacteria (Methylococcus). Bundles of disc-shaped membrane vesicles. Often obligate methylotrophs (lack TCA cycle).
    • Type II: serine pathway. Alphaproteobacteria (Methylosinus). Paired peripheral membranes. Have TCA cycle, more metabolically flexible.

The methane oxidation cascade

CH₄ (−4) → CH₃OH (−2) → HCHO (0) → HCOO⁻ (+2) → CO₂ (+4). 2 e⁻ removed per step, 8 e⁻ total.

  • Step 1 (CH₄ → CH₃OH): methane monooxygenase (MMO). Incorporates one O atom from O₂ into the methyl C; the other O reduced to H₂O using 2 e⁻ from NADH. Genes: pmoA (particulate MMO) or mmoC (soluble MMO).
  • Step 2 (CH₃OH → HCHO): methanol dehydrogenase (periplasmic). Produces NADH.
  • Step 3 (HCHO → HCOOH): formaldehyde dehydrogenase. Produces NADH.
  • Step 4 (HCOOH → CO₂): formate dehydrogenase. Produces NADH.
  • NADH feeds ETC → PMF → ATP via ATP synthase.
MMO is non-specific. MMO also oxidises NH₃; AMO likewise oxidises CH₄. They compete for substrate when both are present. NH₄⁺ is toxic to MMO. This cross-reactivity matters in soils where AOB and methanotrophs coexist.

Anaerobic oxidation of methane (AOM)

CH₄ disappears in anoxic marine sediments. What's the e⁻ acceptor?

  • SO₄²⁻-coupled AOM (classic): a syntrophic consortium of two organisms:
    • ANME (anaerobic methanotrophic archaea) — ANME-1 (Methanomicrobiales), ANME-2 + ANME-3 (Methanosarcinales). Run mcr in reverse to oxidise CH₄.
    • Sulfate-reducing bacteria: Desulfosarcina, Desulfococcus (with ANME-1/-2) or Desulfobulbus (with ANME-3).
    Net: CH₄ + SO₄²⁻ → HCO₃⁻ + HS⁻ + H₂O. Electron transfer mechanism debated (H₂? acetate? direct contact via cytochromes?).
  • Nitrite-coupled AOM: Methylomirabilis oxyfera. Single organism, no partner. Reduces NO₂⁻ → NO, then disproportionates 2 NO → N₂ + O₂. The intracellular O₂ fuels normal aerobic methanotrophy via MMO. Brilliant: makes its own O₂ in anoxic environments.
  • Sulfur disproportionation-based AOM: ANME-2 oxidises CH₄ + reduces SO₄²⁻ to disulfide (HS₂⁻); SRB partner disproportionates HS₂⁻ → HS⁻ + SO₄²⁻.
  • Metal-coupled AOM: Fe(III) or Mn(IV) as acceptor; emerging area.

Exam traps register

Trap 1 — methanogens are Archaea. Not bacteria. Basic-recall trap.
Trap 2 — acetoclastic methanogenesis: one molecule, two C fates. CH₃COOH → CH₄ + CO₂. Methyl C reduced; carboxyl C oxidised. Disproportionation logic within one acetate.
Trap 3 — F₄₂₀ (oxidised) vs F₄₂₀H₂ (reduced). F₄₂₀H₂ donates electrons in methylene → methyl steps. The oxidised form absorbs at 420 nm (gives methanogens their name).
Trap 4 — MMO and AMO cross-react. MMO oxidises NH₃; AMO oxidises CH₄. NH₄⁺ toxic to MMO. Matters in soils.
Trap 5 — methylotroph ≠ methanotroph. Methylotroph: any C₁ substrate (CH₃OH, CH₃NH₂, CH₃SH, CH₄). Methanotroph: specifically CH₄. All methanotrophs are methylotrophs; not the reverse.
Trap 6 — mcr works in both directions. Forward (methanogenesis): CH₃-CoM + HS-CoB → CH₄ + CoM-S-S-CoB. Reverse (AOM): CH₄ + CoM-S-S-CoB → CH₃-CoM + HS-CoB. ANME archaea do the reverse.
Trap 7 — CH₃-H₄MP → CH₃-CoM step pumps Na⁺. Methanogens have Na⁺/H⁺ antiporter to convert this Na⁺ motive force to PMF for ATP synthesis. Energy bookkeeping uses both ion gradients.

INTEG A rice farmer wants to reduce CH₄ emissions. He's advised to keep the water level low. Why might this work, and what could go wrong?

Lowering water level increases O₂ diffusion into soil. But methanotrophy also drops (no substrate). Either way, CH₄ emissions go down. From HW5 P2.

DNRA vs denitrification — the most common exam distinction. Both reduce NO₃⁻. Different outcomes:
  • Denitrification ends at N₂ (gas) → net loss of N from ecosystem to atmosphere. Wastewater treatment goal.
  • DNRA ends at NH₄⁺ → N retained as bioavailable form. Conserves N. Common in C-rich, e⁻-donor-abundant settings.

N₂ fixation — the nitrogenase enzyme

  • Equation: N₂ + 8 e⁻ + 8 H⁺ + 16 ATP → 2 NH₃ + H₂ + 16 ADP + 16 P_i. (8 e⁻ consumed; 6 reduce N₂, 2 reduce H⁺ → H₂ as obligate byproduct.)
  • Two components: Fe protein (dimer, e⁻ donor, reduced by Fd/flavodoxin) and MoFe protein (heterotetramer, contains FeMo cofactor where N₂ binds).
  • Fe-protein cycle repeats 8 times to accumulate 8 e⁻ for one N₂.
  • Both components are extremely O₂-sensitive. Strategies for aerobic / O₂-producing diazotrophs:
    • Slime (Azotobacter): O₂-retarding extracellular slime.
    • Spatial separation (Anabaena): heterocysts — thick-walled differentiated cells lacking PS II (no O₂ production) where nitrogenase resides. Vegetative cells do photosynthesis; glutamine shuttles fixed N out, glutamate brings new N in.
    • Temporal separation (unicellular cyanobacteria like Synechococcus): fix N at night.
    • Rapid O₂ removal: balance O₂ consumption by respiration.

Nitrification — two-step vs comammox

Two-step: AOB do NH₃ + 1.5 O₂ → NO₂⁻ + H₂O + H⁺ (ΔG⁰′ = −277 kJ/mol NH₃). NOB do NO₂⁻ + 0.5 O₂ → NO₃⁻ (ΔG⁰′ = −77 kJ/mol). Combined ΔG = −354 kJ/mol NH₃. Slow-growing, autotrophic (Calvin), aerobic.

Comammox (Daims et al. Nature 2015): Nitrospira inopinata does the full NH₄⁺ + 2 O₂ → NO₃⁻ + H₂O + 2 H⁺ in a single cell.

AOB mechanism: AMO oxidises NH₃ to NH₂OH (hydroxylamine), consuming 2 H⁺ + 2 e⁻ (from HAO). HAO then oxidises NH₂OH → NO₂⁻, releasing 4 e⁻: 2 e⁻ return to AMO; 2 e⁻ flow to cyt aa₃ (reduces O₂ to H₂O) → PMF → ATP. Autotrophic via Calvin.

Anammox — the anammoxosome

Brocadia: NH₄⁺ + NO₂⁻ → N₂ + 2 H₂O. The reaction happens inside a membrane-bounded organelle (anammoxosome) because the intermediate hydrazine (N₂H₄) is highly toxic.

  1. NIR (nitrite reductase): NO₂⁻ + 2 H⁺ + e⁻ → NO + H₂O.
  2. HZS (hydrazine synthase): NO + NH₄⁺ + 2 H⁺ + 3 e⁻ → N₂H₄ + H₂O. The N₂H₄ stays sequestered.
  3. HDH (hydrazine dehydrogenase): N₂H₄ → N₂ + 4 H⁺ + 4 e⁻. These 4 e⁻ go through bc₁ complex → translocate H⁺ → ATP via ATPase.
  4. CO₂ fixation: Nxr (the same enzyme NOB use) runs in reverse: NO₂⁻ → NO₃⁻ + 2 e⁻. Those electrons fuel Wood-Ljungdahl pathway (CODH/acetyl-CoA). So nitrite serves double duty: e⁻ acceptor for anammox catabolism AND e⁻ donor for CO₂ fixation anabolism.

N₂O production — two sources from AOB (slide 48)

  • Chemical decomposition of NH₂OH (unstable intermediate auto-oxidises → NO and N₂O).
  • "Nitrifier denitrification": electrons from AOB ammonia oxidation are diverted to reduce NO₂⁻ → N₂. Same organism, alternative electron route under low-O₂.
  • Plus N₂O and NO production by classical denitrification (incomplete denitrification leaks N₂O before NOS reaches N₂).

Wastewater applications (highly testable)

  • Two-step nitrification + denitrification: nitrify (aerobic, NH₄⁺ → NO₃⁻) then denitrify (anaerobic, NO₃⁻ + methanol → N₂). Energetically expensive (aeration + organic C addition).
  • Nitritation-denitritation: stop oxidation at NO₂⁻ (suppress NOB); denitrify NO₂⁻ → N₂ directly. Saves O₂ (1.5 vs 2 mol O₂/N) and organic C.
  • Anammox-based (DEMON, etc.): AOB convert half NH₄⁺ → NO₂⁻; anammox combines remaining NH₄⁺ + NO₂⁻ → N₂. No organic C needed. State of the art.
  • One-sludge vs two-sludge design (HW6 P4): one-sludge mixes nitrifiers + heterotrophs in one tank; two-sludge has a short tank for heterotrophs (low residence time, removes BOD) followed by a long tank (20-day residence) where slow-growing autotrophic nitrifiers can establish without competition. Two-sludge gives cleaner separation but more infrastructure.

Trap 1 — DNRA vs denitrification. Both reduce NO₃⁻ but end at different products (NH₄⁺ vs N₂). DNRA conserves N; denitrification removes it. Read carefully whether the question is about retention or loss.
Trap 2 — comammox is one organism, NOT a synonym for two-step nitrification. Nitrospira inopinata does both steps. Two-step is two organisms (AOB + NOB).
Trap 3 — nitrogenase needs anaerobic conditions. Even aerobic diazotrophs protect the enzyme. Anabaena uses heterocysts; Synechococcus uses temporal separation; Azotobacter uses slime. If asked "how does Anabaena fix N while doing oxygenic photosynthesis?" → spatial separation in heterocysts (no PS II → no O₂).
Trap 4 — anammox happens in the anammoxosome. Specific membrane-bounded organelle (rare in bacteria!) in Brocadia. Stores toxic hydrazine. The protons translocated across this membrane drive ATP synthase. This is a fingerprint detail.
Trap 5 — Nxr runs in two directions. In NOB: NO₂⁻ → NO₃⁻ (forward, for catabolism). In anammox: NO₂⁻ → NO₃⁻ (still oxidation, but the electrons go to CO₂ fixation, not to terminal O₂ reduction). Same enzyme, different downstream electron destinations.
Trap 6 — Feammox / Surammox / Mnammox (slide 57): coupling NH₄⁺ oxidation to Fe(III), SO₄²⁻, or Mn(IV) reduction. Feammox is the only one with experimental support. These are extra-niche processes — be aware of them; they could be a curveball.
Trap 7 — anaerobic processes can do nitrification kinetically too slowly to win in aerated tanks. Hence two-sludge design (HW6 P4) — give nitrifiers a long-residence tank to outgrow heterotrophs. Slow growth of autotrophic nitrifiers is the design constraint.

Drill bank — Nitrogen cycle

RECALL List five microbial N transformations and their oxidation-state changes.
Answer

(1) N₂ fixation: 0 → −3 (reduction, 6 e⁻). (2) Nitrification: −3 → +5 (oxidation, 8 e⁻ total). (3) Denitrification: +5 → 0 (reduction, 5 e⁻ to N₂). (4) DNRA: +5 → −3 (reduction, 8 e⁻ to NH₄⁺). (5) Anammox: NH₄⁺ (−3) + NO₂⁻ (+3) → N₂ (0); combined oxidation + reduction in one organism.

CONCEPT Distinguish DNRA from denitrification. Why is the distinction ecologically important?
Answer

Both reduce NO₃⁻. Denitrification → N₂ (gas, escapes to atmosphere); net loss of N from ecosystem. DNRA → NH₄⁺ (stays bioavailable); net conservation of N. In high-C, electron-donor-rich anaerobic systems (e.g. organic-rich sediments), DNRA tends to win because it accepts more electrons per nitrate (8 vs 5). In low-C systems, denitrification dominates. Ecologically, DNRA conserves nitrogen for primary producers; denitrification removes nitrogen for the long term. Wastewater treatment exploits denitrification to remove N.

CONCEPT Why does Anabaena need heterocysts to fix N₂?
Answer

Anabaena is a cyanobacterium doing oxygenic photosynthesis — produces O₂. Nitrogenase is irreversibly inactivated by O₂. Solution: differentiate ~5–10 % of cells into heterocysts — thick-walled, lacking PS II (so no O₂ production internally), filled with nitrogenase. Vegetative cells do photosynthesis and ship glutamine (containing fixed N) to heterocysts; heterocysts ship back glutamate with new NH₃ incorporated. Any O₂ that diffuses in is consumed by respiration. Spatial separation of two incompatible chemistries within one organism.

CALC Write the half-reaction for full denitrification of nitrate to N₂. How many electrons per nitrate?
Answer

NO₃⁻ → ½ N₂ (N goes from +5 to 0). That's 5 e⁻ per nitrate.
Balanced: NO₃⁻ + 5 e⁻ + 6 H⁺ → ½ N₂ + 3 H₂O.
For 2 nitrates: 2 NO₃⁻ + 10 e⁻ + 12 H⁺ → N₂ + 6 H₂O.

CALC HW6 P1 scenario: A sealed batch with 10 mM NH₄⁺, 10 mM O₂, neutral pH, no organic C. After 12 h, NH₄⁺ is depleted and NO₃⁻ has built up to ~10 mM. Write the equation. After Fe(II) injection, NO₃⁻ disappears; stoichiometry shows 4 Fe(II) per NO₃⁻. What is the N product?
Answer

Phase 1: nitrification (NH₄⁺ + 2 O₂ → NO₃⁻ + H₂O + 2 H⁺, ΔG⁰′ = −354 kJ/mol). Autotrophic, since no organic C. AOB + NOB.

Phase 2 (Fe(II) injected, no O₂): denitrification-like process. 4 Fe(II) per NO₃⁻ means 4 e⁻ accepted per nitrate. N goes from +5 to +1 = N₂O.
Balanced: 4 Fe²⁺ + NO₃⁻ + 5 H⁺ → 4 Fe³⁺ + ½ N₂O + 2.5 H₂O.

This is autotrophic too (no organic C). Some Fe(II)-oxidising chemodenitrifying microbe at work.

CALC If instead the Fe(II):NO₃⁻ stoichiometry had been 5:1, what product would N be? (HW6 P2 scenario.)
Answer

5 e⁻ per nitrate → N goes from +5 to 0 = N₂. Balanced: 5 Fe²⁺ + NO₃⁻ + 6 H⁺ → 5 Fe³⁺ + ½ N₂ + 3 H₂O. Stoichiometry of Fe(II):NO₃⁻ tells you exactly which N reduction product to expect — count e⁻ from the Fe(II) and divide.

CONCEPT Explain the role of the anammoxosome.
Answer

The anammoxosome is a membrane-bounded compartment inside Brocadia cells where the anammox reaction takes place. Key functions: (1) sequesters the highly toxic intermediate hydrazine (N₂H₄), preventing damage to the rest of the cell; (2) provides a membrane across which the bc₁ complex can translocate H⁺, generating a PMF; (3) houses the ATPase that uses this PMF to make ATP. It's a rare example of a bacterial organelle (most organelles are eukaryotic).

CONCEPT A new bacterium is found that oxidises NH₃ to NO₃⁻ in a single step. What is it called and why is its discovery significant?
Answer

Comammox = "complete ammonia oxidiser". Nitrospira inopinata, discovered in 2015 (Daims et al. Nature). Significance: classical nitrification was thought to require two separate organisms (AOB then NOB) because of the energy distribution between the two steps and slow growth of nitrifiers. Comammox shows that nature has evolved single-organism nitrification despite this — they capture the entire ΔG = −354 kJ/mol NH₃ in one cell. Has implications for: (a) interpretation of soil/water nitrogen cycling community structure; (b) wastewater design; (c) the evolutionary plausibility of intermediate steps in the N cycle.

INTEG Design a wastewater treatment process to remove NH₄⁺ from a high-strength influent (low C). Compare classical N+DN, nitritation-denitritation, and anammox-based options.
Answer

Classical N+DN: nitrify (NH₄⁺ + 2 O₂ → NO₃⁻) → denitrify (NO₃⁻ + methanol → N₂ + CO₂). Pros: well-understood. Cons: high O₂ demand (2 mol/mol N), high organic C demand (for denitrification — methanol added), large footprint.

Nitritation-denitritation: stop at NO₂⁻ (suppress NOB by high T or low DO) → denitrify directly NO₂⁻ → N₂ with reduced organic C input. Pros: 25 % less O₂; less organic C. Cons: harder to control (NOB suppression is finicky).

Anammox-based (DEMON, ANAMMOX): half-nitritation (50 % of NH₄⁺ → NO₂⁻ by AOB) + anammox (remaining NH₄⁺ + NO₂⁻ → N₂). Pros: no organic C needed (anammox bacteria are autotrophic); minimal aeration. Cons: anammox is slow-growing (long startup time); sensitive to O₂ excursions.

For high-strength low-C influent (like sludge digester filtrate), anammox-based wins on cost. For low-strength influent, classical may be more reliable.

M01 · Intro / metabolism

Idea: Every metabolism = 4 things (energy / e⁻ donor / e⁻ acceptor / C source). Nomenclature: energy-electrons-carbon.

Formula: ΔG⁰′ = −n · F · ΔE⁰′, F ≈ 96.5 kJ/(mol·V), ΔE⁰′ = E_acceptor − E_donor.

Trap: Light is the energy source, not the e⁻ donor, in photosynthesis. H₂O is the donor in oxygenic PS.

M02 · Enzymes

Idea: 6 classes: oxidoreductase, transferase, hydrolase, lyase, isomerase, ligase. Identify by what changes in the reaction.

Trap: Transferase vs ligase — both can involve ATP. Transferase: ATP fragment ends up in product. Ligase: ATP only as energy (ADP+Pi or AMP+PPi visible).

Trap: Enzymes don't change ΔG, only Eₐ.

M03 · Kinetics & chemostat

Idea: v = v_max · [S]/(K_M + [S]). At [S] ≪ K_M, v ≈ (v_max/K_M)·[S] (first order). At [S] = K_M, v = v_max/2. At [S] ≫ K_M, v = v_max.

Inhibition Lineweaver-Burk fingerprints: competitive: same y-int, larger −1/K_M. Non-competitive: same x-int, larger y-int. Uncompetitive: parallel shift.

Chemostat formulas: S = K_S·D/(μ_max−D); X = Y·(S₀−S); S_min = b·K_S/(μ_max−b); washout if D ≥ μ_max.

Trap: Methanol MW = 32, not 16. Use b directly in S_min. At D = μ_max/2, outlet S = K_S.

M04 · Fermentation

Idea: Organic donor = organic acceptor (intramolecular). SLP only. ~2 ATP/glucose. No external acceptor.

Products signature → organism:
2 lactate → Lactobacillus
2 EtOH + 2 CO₂ → yeast/Zymomonas
butyrate + 2 CO₂ + 2 H₂ → Clostridium
propionate + acetate + CO₂ (from lactate) → Propionibacterium

Trap: Propionic fermentation is secondary — needs lactate first. ABE in Clostridium = late, pH-drop phase.

M05 · Fe(III) reduction

Idea: Fe(III) is insoluble at pH 7 — must transfer e⁻ to a solid surface. 5 strategies: direct contact, nanowires, shuttles, chelators, biofilm hopping.

Organisms: Shewanella on lactate (4 e⁻, → acetate, not CO₂; needs direct contact); Geobacter on acetate (8 e⁻, → 2 CO₂; nanowires + cytochromes).

Trap: Pyruvate ferments to H₂ (diffusible) — works with embedded Fe(III). Lactate doesn't — needs direct contact. ratio 1:4 (Shewanella/lactate) vs 1:8 (Geobacter/acetate).

M06 · Fe(II) oxidation

Idea: 3 groups by pH/O₂: acidophilic aerobic (A. ferrooxidans), neutrophilic aerobic (Gallionella, Leptothrix), anaerobic neutrophilic (phototrophic R. palustris, NO₃⁻-reducing Ferroglobus).

The diagram: A. ferrooxidans dual ETC. Downhill: Fe²⁺ → rusticyanin (+0.68) → cyt c → cyt aa₃ → O₂ (small ΔE, makes ATP). Uphill (reverse e⁻ flow): Fe²⁺ → cyt bc₁ → Q → Fe-S → flavoprotein → NAD⁺/NADH (PMF-driven). For CO₂ fixation (Calvin).

Trap: Fe(II)/Fe(III) E⁰′ depends on pH: +0.76 V at pH 2; +0.1–0.2 V at pH 7. Don't reuse.

M07 · Photosynthesis

Idea: Light excites pigment → P* (better e⁻ donor). Two RC types: RC I reduces ferredoxin (linear, makes NADH directly); RC II reduces quinone (cyclic, needs reverse e⁻ flow for NADH).

Map: Cyanobacteria = PS I + PS II (Z scheme), H₂O donor, O₂ producer, Calvin. Green sulfur = RC I, H₂S donor, rTCA. Green non-sulfur = RC II, 3-HP bi-cycle. Purple bacteria = RC II, Calvin, H₂/H₂S/organic donors.

Trap: "Green" means colour, not RC type. Green sulfur has RC I, green non-sulfur has RC II.

M08 · CO₂ fixation

The 6 pathways → organisms:
Calvin = cyanobacteria, plants, purple, A. ferrooxidans, nitrifiers (key enzyme: RubisCO)
rTCA = green sulfur (Chlorobium), some SRB (key: ATP-citrate lyase)
Wood-Ljungdahl = acetogens, methanogens, anammox (key: CODH; strict anaerobes only)
3-HP bi-cycle = green non-sulfur (Chloroflexus)
3-HP/4-HB = aerobic archaea (Sulfolobales)
DC/4-HB = anaerobic archaea (Thermoproteales)

Trap: Don't memorise intermediate names (teacher said). Focus on C counts, key enzymes, organisms, O₂ tolerance.

M09 · Carbon cycling / methane

Idea: Methanogenesis (3 types) by Archaea. Methanotrophy by Bacteria (Type I RuMP / Type II serine). AOM by ANME + SRB consortium or M. oxyfera (makes own O₂).

The diagnostic gene: mcr (methyl-CoM reductase) — last step of all methanogenesis types AND first step (reverse) of ANME AOM.

Trap: Methanogens are Archaea, not Bacteria. Methylotrophs ⊃ methanotrophs (methanotroph eats CH₄ specifically). MMO and AMO cross-react with CH₄ and NH₃.

M10 · Nitrogen cycle

Idea: 5 processes mapped to ox states.
Aerobic: nitrification (AOB + NOB or comammox N. inopinata).
Anaerobic: denitrification (NO₃⁻ → N₂, loss); DNRA (NO₃⁻ → NH₄⁺, retention); anammox (NH₄⁺ + NO₂⁻ → N₂).

The diagrams: Anammoxosome organelle in Brocadia (toxic hydrazine sequestered). Nxr runs reverse for CO₂ fixation. Heterocysts in Anabaena spatially segregate nitrogenase from O₂.

Trap: DNRA vs denitrification — both reduce NO₃⁻, opposite ecological consequence. mcr is for methanogenesis/AOM, not N cycle — don't confuse.

One-line memory rules

  • Acceptor on top, donor on bottom (redox tower) → ΔE positive → favourable
  • SLP = no PMF = fermentation; oxidative phosphorylation = ETC + PMF + ATP synthase
  • Counter ATPs and NADHs across pathways; Calvin is expensive (7 ATP / pyr), WL is cheap (~1)
  • Strict anaerobes use CODH (Wood-Ljungdahl); O₂ destroys it
  • Decaheme c-type cytochrome on outer membrane = metal reducer (Shewanella, Geobacter)
  • Reverse electron flow = high-E donor (Fe²⁺, NO₂⁻, NH₃) needs to make NADH
  • Carboxysome = cyanobacterial RubisCO concentrator
  • Anammoxosome = Brocadia organelle for hydrazine
  • Comammox = single organism nitrification (Nitrospira inopinata)
  • Disproportionation = one compound on left, two divergent ox states on right
